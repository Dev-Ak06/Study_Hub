3.03 Statistical Mechanics

Statistical mechanics is a branch of physics that explains the macroscopic properties of a system using the statistical behaviour of its microscopic particles.

It connects the behaviour of atoms, molecules and other particles with measurable quantities such as temperature, pressure and energy.

1. Concept of Energy Levels and Energy States

Energy Level

An energy level is an allowed value of energy that a quantum system can possess. In many quantum systems, energy is quantized, meaning that only certain energy values are permitted.

Energy State

An energy state describes a particular quantum state of a system. Different quantum states can sometimes have the same energy. Such states are called degenerate states.

Example:

An atom may have a ground state with the lowest allowed energy and excited states with higher energies.

Important Terms

  • Ground state: the lowest-energy state.
  • Excited state: a state with energy higher than the ground state.
  • Energy difference: the difference between two allowed energy levels.
  • Degeneracy: more than one state having the same energy.

2. Phase Space

Phase space is a mathematical space used to represent the position and momentum of a particle or system.

For one particle moving along the x-axis, a point in phase space is specified by its position x and momentum p.

Phase-space coordinates: (x, p)

For a particle moving in three dimensions, the coordinates are:

(x, y, z, pₓ, pᵧ, p_z)

Therefore, the phase space of one particle in three dimensions has six dimensions: three position coordinates and three momentum coordinates.

Importance of Phase Space

  • It represents the possible states of a physical system.
  • It helps describe the motion of particles.
  • It is useful in classical and statistical mechanics.
  • A point represents a specified position and momentum.

3. Microstates and Macrostates

Microstate

A microstate is a detailed description of the microscopic state of a system, including the states occupied by its individual particles.

Macrostate

A macrostate describes a system using macroscopic quantities such as total energy, pressure, volume and temperature.

Example: Two Particles in Two Energy Levels

Consider two distinguishable particles, A and B, and two energy levels, E₀ and E₁.

Possible arrangements include:

  • A in E₀ and B in E₀.
  • A in E₀ and B in E₁.
  • A in E₁ and B in E₀.
  • A in E₁ and B in E₁.

The detailed arrangements are microstates. A macrostate groups microscopic arrangements according to specified macroscopic conditions, such as the total energy.

Difference Between Microstate and Macrostate

Microstate Macrostate
Detailed microscopic arrangement. Overall macroscopic condition.
Describes individual particle states. Describes quantities such as energy and temperature.
Several microstates may correspond to one macrostate. Can represent many possible microstates.

4. Thermodynamic Probability

Thermodynamic probability, commonly represented by W or Ω, is the number of microscopic arrangements corresponding to a particular macrostate.

It is a count of possible arrangements, not an ordinary probability between zero and one.

Formula for Maxwell–Boltzmann Arrangements

For N distinguishable particles distributed among energy levels, with nᵢ particles in level i and degeneracy gᵢ, the number of arrangements is:

W = N! / (n₁! n₂! n₃! ...)

This formula applies to the distribution of distinguishable particles among specified groups. If the energy levels have degeneracies, the corresponding state multiplicities must also be included.

Example

Three distinguishable particles are distributed between two energy levels. If two particles are in the first level and one is in the second level:

W = 3! / (2! 1!)

W = 6 / 2 = 3

Answer: 3 arrangements.

Connection with Entropy

Boltzmann's entropy formula connects thermodynamic probability with entropy:

S = kB ln W

Where:

  • S = entropy
  • kB = Boltzmann constant = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K
  • W = thermodynamic probability
  • ln = natural logarithm

5. Maxwell–Boltzmann (MB) Statistics

Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics describes the distribution of classical, distinguishable particles among available energy states.

It is suitable when quantum effects are negligible, such as in many ordinary dilute gases at sufficiently high temperatures.

Main Characteristics

  • Particles are treated as distinguishable.
  • There is no quantum restriction limiting the number of particles in a state.
  • It is commonly used for classical ideal gases.
  • It is a good approximation in the classical dilute-gas limit.

Distribution Formula

For particles distributed among energy levels, the MB occupation is proportional to:

nᵢ ∝ gᵢ e−Eᵢ/(kBT)

The normalized average occupation per single-particle state is:

fMB(E) = e−(E−μ)/(kBT)

Here μ is the chemical potential. In the classical limit, the occupation per individual state is much smaller than one.

Boltzmann Factor

Boltzmann factor = e−E/(kBT)

Higher-energy states are generally less populated at thermal equilibrium than lower-energy states, all else being equal.

6. Bose–Einstein (BE) Statistics

Bose–Einstein statistics describes indistinguishable particles called bosons.

Bosons have integer spin and are not restricted by the Pauli exclusion principle. Multiple bosons can occupy the same single-particle quantum state.

Examples of Bosons

  • Photons
  • Gluons
  • Helium-4 atoms, treated as composite bosons in suitable conditions

BE Distribution Formula

The average occupation number of a single-particle state with energy E is:

fBE(E) = 1 / [e(E−μ)/(kBT) − 1]

Where:

  • E = energy of the state
  • μ = chemical potential
  • kB = Boltzmann constant
  • T = absolute temperature

Main Characteristics

  • Bosons are indistinguishable particles.
  • Any number of bosons may occupy one quantum state.
  • BE statistics explains the distribution of photons in black-body radiation.
  • It is important in phenomena such as Bose–Einstein condensation.

7. Fermi–Dirac (FD) Statistics

Fermi–Dirac statistics describes indistinguishable particles called fermions.

Fermions have half-integer spin and obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Each individual quantum state can be occupied by at most one fermion.

Examples of Fermions

  • Electrons
  • Protons
  • Neutrons

FD Distribution Formula

The average occupation number of a single-particle state with energy E is:

fFD(E) = 1 / [e(E−μ)/(kBT) + 1]

The occupation probability of a single state lies between zero and one.

Main Characteristics

  • Fermions are indistinguishable particles.
  • Each single-particle state can contain at most one fermion.
  • Electrons in solids follow FD statistics.
  • It helps explain the behaviour of electrons in metals and semiconductors.
