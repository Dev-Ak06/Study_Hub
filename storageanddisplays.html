Module 5: Storage and Display Devices

Storage devices are used to store digital information for future use. Display devices convert electrical signals into visual information that humans can see.

This module covers magnetic storage materials and the operation and applications of CRT, CRO, LED and OLED.

1. Different Storage Devices

Definition

A storage device is a hardware component used to record, retain and retrieve digital data.

Types of Storage Devices

Storage Device Working Principle Examples
Magnetic Storage Stores information using magnetic states. Hard disk drive, magnetic tape
Optical Storage Uses a laser to read data encoded on a disc. CD, DVD, Blu-ray disc
Semiconductor Storage Stores information electronically in semiconductor devices. SSD, pen drive, memory card
Primary Memory Provides fast access to data used by the processor. RAM, ROM

Important Storage Devices

1. Hard Disk Drive (HDD):

An HDD stores data on rotating magnetic platters. A read/write head accesses the data. It is commonly used for computer storage and backups.

2. Magnetic Tape:

Magnetic tape stores information on a magnetically coated strip. It is mainly used for long-term backups and archival storage.

3. Solid-State Drive (SSD):

An SSD stores data in flash memory. It has no moving mechanical parts and is commonly used in laptops and desktop computers.

4. Optical Disc:

Optical discs use laser light to read encoded data. CDs, DVDs and Blu-ray discs are examples.

5. RAM and ROM:

  • RAM is generally volatile memory, meaning its data is lost when power is removed.
  • ROM is non-volatile memory used to retain information without continuous power.

2. Magnetic Storage Materials

Definition

Magnetic storage materials are materials used to store information by changing or detecting their magnetic states.

In magnetic storage, digital data is represented by different magnetic configurations corresponding to binary information.

Important Magnetic Properties

1. Magnetization:

Magnetization describes the magnetic moment per unit volume of a material.

2. Magnetic Hysteresis:

Hysteresis is the dependence of magnetization on the previous magnetic history of a material.

3. Retentivity:

Retentivity is the ability of a material to retain magnetization after the external magnetizing field is removed.

4. Coercivity:

Coercivity is the reverse magnetic field required to reduce the magnetization to zero after magnetization.

Magnetic Storage Materials

  • Iron oxide: Used in magnetic recording media, including some magnetic tapes.
  • Cobalt-based alloys: Used in certain high-density magnetic recording applications.
  • Iron-platinum alloys: Studied and used in specialised high-density magnetic recording technologies.
  • Magnetic thin films: Thin magnetic layers used in recording media and data-storage technologies.

Operation of Magnetic Storage

  1. A storage medium contains a layer or region made of magnetic material.
  2. During writing, a write head produces a magnetic field that changes the magnetic state of a small region.
  3. Different magnetic states represent encoded binary data.
  4. During reading, a read sensor detects magnetic changes or states.
  5. Electronic circuits convert the detected signals into digital data.

Applications

  • Hard disk drives
  • Magnetic tapes for backups
  • Long-term data archives
  • Magnetic recording systems

Advantages

  • Suitable for storing large quantities of data.
  • Magnetic storage can retain data without continuous electrical power.
  • Magnetic tape can be economical for archival storage.

Limitations

  • Some devices, such as HDDs, contain moving mechanical parts.
  • Strong magnetic fields can affect certain magnetic media.
  • Mechanical devices can wear out over time.

3. Cathode Ray Tube (CRT)

Definition

A Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) is an electronic display device that produces images by directing a beam of electrons onto a phosphor-coated screen.

Main Parts of a CRT

  • Electron gun: Produces a beam of electrons.
  • Control grid: Controls the intensity of the electron beam.
  • Focusing system: Focuses the electron beam.
  • Deflection system: Changes the direction of the beam.
  • Phosphor screen: Emits light when struck by electrons.
  • Vacuum envelope: Allows electrons to travel with little interference from gas molecules.

Operation of CRT

  1. The heated cathode in the electron gun emits electrons.
  2. The electrons are accelerated to form an electron beam.
  3. The focusing system makes the beam narrow.
  4. The deflection system directs the beam to different positions on the screen.
  5. When electrons strike the phosphor coating, it emits visible light.
  6. By controlling the beam's position and intensity, an image is formed.

Applications of CRT

  • Older television sets
  • Older computer monitors
  • Oscilloscopes
  • Some specialised display and laboratory equipment

Advantages

  • Can provide good motion response.
  • Historically offered good image quality for its time.

Limitations

  • Large and heavy construction.
  • Higher power consumption than many modern displays.
  • Uses a high-voltage electron-beam system.

4. Cathode Ray Oscilloscope (CRO)

Definition

A Cathode Ray Oscilloscope (CRO) is an electronic instrument that displays electrical signals as waveforms on a screen.

A conventional CRO uses a CRT as its display tube.

Main Parts of a CRO

  • CRT screen and electron gun
  • Vertical amplifier
  • Horizontal amplifier
  • Time-base generator
  • Trigger circuit
  • Power supply

Operation of CRO

  1. The input electrical signal is applied to the vertical channel.
  2. The vertical amplifier increases the signal to a suitable level.
  3. The signal controls the vertical movement of the electron beam.
  4. The time-base generator produces a sweep voltage that moves the beam horizontally with time.
  5. The combined horizontal and vertical movements produce a waveform on the screen.
  6. The waveform can be studied to determine amplitude, time period and frequency.

Applications of CRO

  • Observing electrical waveforms
  • Measuring voltage and time period
  • Determining frequency
  • Comparing the phase of two signals
  • Testing and troubleshooting electronic circuits

Important CRO Formulas

Voltage = Vertical divisions × Volts per division

Time period T = Horizontal divisions for one cycle × Time per division

Frequency f = 1/T

These formulas assume the displayed waveform and scale settings are interpreted correctly.

5. Light Emitting Diode (LED)

Definition

A Light Emitting Diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that emits light when it is forward biased and current flows through it.

Principle

An LED works on the principle of electroluminescence. When electrons recombine with holes in the semiconductor, energy may be released as photons.

The colour of the emitted light depends mainly on the semiconductor's energy band gap.

Construction

  • Semiconductor p-n junction
  • Electrical contacts
  • Protective package
  • Transparent or coloured encapsulation, depending on the design

Operation of LED

  1. The LED is connected in forward bias using a suitable current-limiting arrangement.
  2. Electrons and holes move towards the junction region.
  3. Electrons recombine with holes.
  4. Energy is released, and in a suitable semiconductor, some of it appears as photons.
  5. The photons leave the device as visible or other electromagnetic light, depending on the LED type.

Applications of LED

  • Indicator lights
  • Traffic signals
  • Lighting systems
  • Digital displays
  • Remote-control and optical communication systems, where appropriate LED types are used

Advantages of LED

  • Low power consumption compared with many traditional light sources.
  • Long operating life under suitable conditions.
  • Compact size.
  • Fast switching.

Limitations

  • Requires correct polarity and suitable current control.
  • Excess current can damage the device.
  • Heat management may be needed in high-power LEDs.

6. Organic Light Emitting Diode (OLED)

Definition

An Organic Light Emitting Diode (OLED) is a light-emitting device that uses organic semiconductor materials to produce light when an electric current passes through it.

Construction

  • Substrate, which supports the device
  • Anode
  • Organic hole-transport and emitting layers
  • Cathode
  • Protective encapsulation

The exact layer structure varies between OLED designs.

Operation of OLED

  1. A voltage is applied across the anode and cathode.
  2. Holes are injected from the anode and electrons from the cathode.
  3. These charge carriers move through the organic layers.
  4. Electrons and holes combine in the emitting region.
  5. Energy is released as photons, producing light.

Applications of OLED

  • Smartphone displays
  • Television screens
  • Smartwatches
  • Flexible displays
  • Some lighting applications

Advantages of OLED

  • Each pixel can emit its own light.
  • Dark pixels can be switched off, allowing deep blacks.
  • Can be manufactured in thin and flexible forms.
  • Often provides wide viewing angles.

Limitations

  • Some OLED materials can degrade with use.
