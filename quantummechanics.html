3.02 Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that studies the behaviour of microscopic particles such as electrons, atoms and photons. It explains phenomena that cannot be fully explained by classical physics.

This chapter covers wave functions, probability interpretation, normalization, the uncertainty principle and the Schrödinger wave equation.

1. Concept of Wave Function

A wave function is a mathematical function used to describe the quantum state of a particle.

It is generally represented by the Greek letter psi (ψ). For a particle moving in one dimension, the wave function may be written as:

ψ(x,t)

Where:

  • ψ = wave function
  • x = position of the particle
  • t = time

The wave function contains information about the possible results of measurements performed on a quantum particle.

Important Properties of a Wave Function

  • It should be single-valued.
  • It should be finite and continuous for physically acceptable states.
  • It must be normalizable for a normalizable bound state.
  • Its absolute square |ψ|² gives the probability density.

A wave function itself is generally not directly measurable. Physical predictions are obtained from quantities calculated using it.

2. Physical Significance of Wave Function

The wave function ψ does not directly represent the probability. The physically meaningful quantity is its absolute square, |ψ|².

Probability density = |ψ|²

For a real-valued wave function:

|ψ|² = ψ²

For a complex-valued wave function:

|ψ|² = ψ*ψ

Here ψ* is the complex conjugate of ψ.

Meaning

A larger value of |ψ|² at a particular position means a greater probability density of finding the particle near that position.

A smaller value means a lower probability density. If |ψ|² = 0 at a position, the probability density at that position is zero.

Probability in a Small Interval

The probability of finding a particle between x and x + dx is:

dP = |ψ(x)|² dx

The probability of finding a particle between positions a and b is:

P = ∫ₐᵇ |ψ(x)|² dx

The probability is always between 0 and 1.

3. Probability Interpretation of Wave Function

According to Max Born's probability interpretation, the absolute square of the wave function gives the probability density of finding a particle.

Probability density = |ψ|²

For a particle in three-dimensional space:

Probability = ∫ |ψ|² dV

For a particular region, the integration is performed over that region.

Example

Suppose the probability density is constant and equal to 0.5 m⁻¹ in a one-dimensional interval of length 1 metre.

Probability = probability density × interval length

P = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5

Therefore, the probability of finding the particle in that interval is 0.5 or 50%, provided the stated density is part of a valid normalized distribution.

Important Points

  • Probability cannot be negative.
  • The total probability of finding a particle somewhere must be 1.
  • |ψ|² is always real and non-negative.
  • The wave function may be complex.

4. Normalization of Wave Function

Normalization means ensuring that the total probability of finding a particle over all space is equal to 1.

∫₋∞⁺∞ |ψ(x)|² dx = 1

For a three-dimensional wave function:

∫ |ψ|² dV = 1

These equations are called normalization conditions.

Why Is Normalization Necessary?

A particle must be found somewhere in the complete space when its position is measured. Therefore, the total probability must be 1.

If a wave function is not normalized, it can often be multiplied by a suitable constant to obtain a normalized wave function.

Normalization Constant

Suppose:

ψ(x) = A f(x)

Here A is a constant. Applying the normalization condition:

∫ |A f(x)|² dx = 1

For a constant A:

|A|² ∫ |f(x)|² dx = 1

Therefore:

|A|² = 1 / ∫ |f(x)|² dx

For a real positive normalization constant:

A = 1 / √(∫ |f(x)|² dx)

This is the general method for finding a normalization constant.

5. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that the position and momentum of a quantum particle cannot both be known with unlimited precision at the same time.

The more precisely the position is specified, the greater the minimum uncertainty in momentum, and vice versa.

Mathematical Expression

Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2

Since ℏ = h/(2π), the equation can also be written as:

Δx Δp ≥ h/(4π)

Where:

  • Δx = uncertainty in position
  • Δp = uncertainty in momentum
  • h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s
  • ℏ = reduced Planck's constant = h/(2π)

For Position and Velocity

For a non-relativistic particle of mass m:

Δp = m Δv

Therefore:

Δx Δv ≥ h/(4πm)

For Energy and Time

ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2 = h/(4π)

This is the commonly used energy-time uncertainty relation.

Physical Significance

  • It is a fundamental property of quantum systems.
  • It is not simply caused by poor measuring instruments.
  • It is important for microscopic particles such as electrons.
  • It does not mean that all physical quantities are uncertain by the same amount.

6. Introduction to Schrödinger Wave Equation

The Schrödinger wave equation is a fundamental equation of quantum mechanics. It describes how the quantum state of a system evolves.

For a non-relativistic particle of mass m moving in a potential energy V, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is:

iℏ (∂ψ/∂t) = -(ℏ²/2m) ∇²ψ + Vψ

Where:

  • i = imaginary unit
  • ℏ = reduced Planck's constant
  • ψ = wave function
  • m = mass of the particle
  • V = potential energy
  • ∇² = Laplacian operator

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

For a time-independent potential and an energy eigenstate, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is:

-(ℏ²/2m) ∇²ψ + Vψ = Eψ

In one dimension, it becomes:

-(ℏ²/2m)(d²ψ/dx²) + Vψ = Eψ

Here E represents the total energy of the energy eigenstate.

Exam note: Your syllabus requires only the statement and introduction of the Schrödinger wave equation. Detailed derivation is not included here.

7. Important Formula Summary

Concept Formula

Use h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and ℏ ≈ 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J s unless another value is specified.

8. Solved Numerical Problems

These 10 examples cover probability, normalization, uncertainty relations and the Schrödinger equation's basic context.

Numerical 1: Calculate Probability Density

Question: A real wave function has the value ψ = 3 m⁻¹ᐟ² at a particular position. Calculate the probability density.

Formula: |ψ|² = ψ²

Solution:

|ψ|² = 3² = 9 m⁻¹

Answer: 9 m⁻¹

Numerical 2: Probability in an Interval

Question: A particle has a constant probability density of 0.25 m⁻¹ over an interval of length 2 m. Find the probability of finding the particle in this interval.

Formula: P = probability density × length

Solution:

P = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5

Answer: P = 0.5 or 50%

Numerical 3: Find the Normalization Constant

Question: A wave function is ψ(x) = A for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2 m and zero elsewhere. Find A.

Formula: ∫ |ψ|² dx = 1

Solution:

∫₀² A² dx = 1

A² [x]₀² = 1

2A² = 1

A² = 1/2

A = 1/√2 m⁻¹ᐟ²

Answer: A = 1/√2 m⁻¹ᐟ²

Numerical 4: Uncertainty in Momentum

Question: The uncertainty in the position of an electron is 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Find its minimum uncertainty in momentum.

Given: h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s

Formula: Δp ≥ h/(4πΔx)

Solution:

Δp ≥ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 10⁻¹⁰)

Δp ≥ 5.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s

Answer: Minimum Δp ≈ 5.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s

Numerical 5: Uncertainty in Position

Question: The minimum uncertainty in an electron's momentum is 1 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m/s. Calculate the minimum uncertainty in position.

Formula: Δx ≥ h/(4πΔp)

Solution:

Δx ≥ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 10⁻²⁴)

Δx ≥ 5.27 × 10⁻¹¹ m

Answer: Minimum Δx ≈ 5.27 × 10⁻¹¹ m

Numerical 6: Uncertainty in Velocity

Question: An electron's position uncertainty is 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Find the minimum uncertainty in velocity.

Given: electron mass m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg

Formula: Δv ≥ h/(4πmΔx)

Solution:

Δv ≥ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴) / (4π × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2 × 10⁻¹⁰)

Δv ≥ 2.90 × 10⁵ m/s

Answer: Minimum Δv ≈ 2.90 × 10⁵ m/s

Numerical 7: Energy Uncertainty

Question: A quantum state has a characteristic time interval of 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ s. Estimate the energy-time uncertainty scale using ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2.

Formula: ΔE ≥ ℏ/(2Δt)

Solution:

ΔE ≥ (1.055 × 10⁻³⁴)/(2 × 10⁻¹⁵)

ΔE ≥ 5.275 × 10⁻²⁰ J

Answer: ΔE ≥ 5.275 × 10⁻²⁰ J

Numerical 8: Convert Energy into Electron Volts

Question: Convert an energy uncertainty of 3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹ J into electron volts.

Given: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Formula: Energy in eV = Energy in joules / (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)

Solution:

E = (3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)

E = 2 eV

Answer: 2 eV

Numerical 9: Momentum Uncertainty from Velocity Uncertainty

Question: An electron has a velocity uncertainty of 2 × 10⁵ m/s. Calculate its momentum uncertainty.

Given: m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg

Formula: Δp = mΔv

Solution:

Δp = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2 × 10⁵

Δp = 1.822 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s

Answer: Δp = 1.822 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s

Numerical 10: Check the Uncertainty Principle

Question: A particle has position uncertainty Δx = 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m and momentum uncertainty Δp = 3 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m/s. Check whether these values satisfy the uncertainty principle.

Condition: Δx Δp ≥ h/(4π)

Solution:

Left side = (2 × 10⁻¹⁰)(3 × 10⁻²⁵)

Left side = 6 × 10⁻³⁵ J s

Right side = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π)

Right side ≈ 5.27 × 10⁻³⁵ J s

Since 6 × 10⁻³⁵ ≥ 5.27 × 10⁻³⁵, the condition is satisfied.

Answer: Yes, the values satisfy the uncertainty principle.

9. Quick Revision

  • Wave function is represented by ψ.
  • |ψ|² represents probability density.
  • Total probability over all space must be 1.
  • Normalization ensures the total probability equals 1.
  • Δx Δp ≥ h/(4π) is the position-momentum uncertainty relation.
  • ΔE Δt ≥ h/(4π) is the commonly used energy-time relation.
  • The Schrödinger equation describes quantum-state evolution.
  • For this syllabus, learn the Schrödinger equation's statement, not its derivation.
