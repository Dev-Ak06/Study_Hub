Semiconductor

This chapter covers semiconductor physics, electrons and holes, energy bands, metals, insulators, semiconductors, intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors, and the P-N junction.

1. Physics of Semiconductors

Definition

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator.

Examples: Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge).

Important properties

  • Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators.
  • Their conductivity generally increases with temperature.
  • Conductivity can be controlled by doping.
  • They are used in diodes, transistors, solar cells and integrated circuits.

Covalent bonding in silicon

Silicon has four valence electrons. In a silicon crystal, each silicon atom shares its four valence electrons with four neighbouring atoms, forming covalent bonds.

At very low temperatures, an ideal pure semiconductor has very few free charge carriers. When thermal energy breaks a covalent bond, a free electron and a hole are produced.

2. Electrons and Holes

Electron

An electron is a negatively charged particle. When an electron gains sufficient energy, it can leave a covalent bond and move through the crystal.

Charge of an electron = -1.602 x 10^-19 C

Hole

A hole is the absence of an electron in a covalent bond. It behaves like a positive charge carrier in a semiconductor.

Charge of a hole = +1.602 x 10^-19 C

Electron-hole pair

When thermal energy breaks a covalent bond, one free electron and one hole are created. They are called an electron-hole pair.

Recombination

When a free electron fills a hole, the electron and hole disappear as mobile charge carriers. This process is called recombination.

Generation

The creation of an electron-hole pair is called generation.

Important points

  • Electrons carry negative charge.
  • Holes behave as positive charge carriers.
  • Both electrons and holes contribute to current in semiconductors.
  • In a pure semiconductor at thermal equilibrium, electron and hole concentrations are equal.

3. Energy Band Theory

In a solid, the energy levels of many closely spaced atoms form energy bands.

Valence band

The valence band contains electrons involved in bonding. At absolute zero, it is completely filled in an ideal intrinsic semiconductor.

Conduction band

The conduction band contains energy states in which electrons can move through the material and contribute to electrical current.

Forbidden energy gap

The forbidden energy gap is the energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in which no allowed electronic states exist in the ideal band model.

Eg = Ec - Ev

Where:

  • Eg = energy band gap
  • Ec = conduction-band edge energy
  • Ev = valence-band edge energy

5. Intrinsic Semiconductor

Definition

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor without intentionally added impurity atoms.

Examples: Pure silicon and pure germanium.

Working

  1. Silicon atoms form covalent bonds with neighbouring atoms.
  2. Thermal energy can break some of these bonds.
  3. Each broken bond produces one free electron and one hole.
  4. Both electrons and holes can contribute to electrical current.

Carrier concentration

In an intrinsic semiconductor:

n = p = ni

  • n = electron concentration
  • p = hole concentration
  • ni = intrinsic carrier concentration

Mass-action law

At thermal equilibrium, for a non-degenerate semiconductor:

n x p = ni^2

This relation connects electron concentration, hole concentration and intrinsic carrier concentration.

6. Extrinsic Semiconductor

Definition

An extrinsic semiconductor is a semiconductor whose electrical properties have been modified by adding a controlled amount of impurity atoms. This process is called doping.

There are two main types:

  • N-type semiconductor
  • P-type semiconductor

Doping

Doping is the process of adding a small, controlled amount of impurity to a pure semiconductor to increase or control its electrical conductivity.

N-type semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is formed by doping silicon or germanium with a pentavalent impurity having five valence electrons.

Examples of donor impurities: Phosphorus, arsenic and antimony.

Four electrons of the impurity atom form covalent bonds with neighbouring atoms. The fifth electron can become available for conduction.

  • Majority carriers: electrons
  • Minority carriers: holes
  • Donor impurities supply electrons.

P-type semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is formed by doping silicon or germanium with a trivalent impurity having three valence electrons.

Examples of acceptor impurities: Boron, aluminium and gallium.

The impurity atom forms three covalent bonds. The incomplete bonding can accept an electron, effectively producing a hole that can participate in conduction.

  • Majority carriers: holes
  • Minority carriers: electrons
  • Acceptor impurities accept electrons.

Comparison: P-type and N-type

Property N-type P-type
Impurity Pentavalent Trivalent
Examples Phosphorus, arsenic Boron, aluminium
Majority carriers Electrons Holes
Minority carriers Holes Electrons

Important: Both P-type and N-type semiconductors are electrically neutral overall. Their names do not mean that the entire material has a net positive or negative charge.

7. P-N Junction

Definition

A P-N junction is the boundary region formed when P-type and N-type semiconductor regions are joined in a single crystal. It is the basic structure of a semiconductor diode.

Formation of the depletion region

  1. Electrons diffuse from the N-side towards the P-side.
  2. Holes diffuse from the P-side towards the N-side.
  3. Near the junction, electrons and holes recombine.
  4. This leaves fixed ionised donor and acceptor atoms near the junction.
  5. The region depleted of mobile carriers is called the depletion region.
  6. An internal electric field and potential barrier develop.

This is a simplified diagram. The depletion region contains fixed charged ions, not a complete absence of atoms.

Potential barrier

The potential barrier opposes further diffusion of majority carriers across the junction. Its value depends on the semiconductor material, doping and temperature.

Forward bias

In forward bias, the P-side is connected to the positive terminal and the N-side to the negative terminal of the external supply.

  • The potential barrier is reduced.
  • The depletion region becomes narrower.
  • Majority carriers can cross the junction more easily.
  • The diode current increases significantly once forward conduction is established.

Reverse bias

In reverse bias, the P-side is connected to the negative terminal and the N-side to the positive terminal of the external supply.

  • The potential barrier increases.
  • The depletion region becomes wider.
  • Majority-carrier current is strongly suppressed.
  • A small reverse saturation current generally flows due to minority carriers.

Forward bias vs reverse bias

Property Forward bias Reverse bias
P-side connection Positive Negative
N-side connection Negative Positive
Depletion width Decreases Increases
Current Large forward current after conduction begins Small reverse current before breakdown

Applications of a P-N junction diode

  • Rectification of AC into DC
  • Signal detection
  • Electronic switching
  • Protection circuits

8. Important Formulas

  1. Energy gap: Eg = Ec - Ev
  2. Intrinsic semiconductor: n = p = ni
  3. Mass-action law: n x p = ni^2
  4. Intrinsic carrier concentration: ni = square root of (n x p)
  5. Conductivity: sigma = q(n x mun + p x mup)

Where:

  • sigma = electrical conductivity
  • q = magnitude of electronic charge
  • n = electron concentration
  • p = hole concentration
  • mun = electron mobility
  • mup = hole mobility

Conductivity formula explained

Both electrons and holes carry current. Their contributions to conductivity add together:

sigma = q(n x mun + p x mup)

This formula assumes the usual drift-conduction model and uses carrier concentrations per unit volume.

9. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Energy gap

Question: The conduction-band edge is 1.1 eV above the valence-band edge. Find the energy gap.

Given: Ec - Ev = 1.1 eV

Formula: Eg = Ec - Ev

Solution: Eg = 1.1 eV

Answer: 1.1 eV

Numerical 2: Convert electron volts to joules

Question: Convert an energy gap of 1.1 eV into joules.

Given: 1 eV = 1.602 x 10^-19 J

Formula: Energy in joules = energy in eV x 1.602 x 10^-19

Solution:

Eg = 1.1 x 1.602 x 10^-19 J

Eg = 1.7622 x 10^-19 J

Answer: 1.7622 x 10^-19 J

Numerical 3: Intrinsic carrier concentration

Question: An intrinsic semiconductor has electron concentration 3 x 10^16 m^-3. Find the hole concentration.

Given: n = ni = 3 x 10^16 m^-3

Formula: n = p = ni

Solution: p = 3 x 10^16 m^-3

Answer: 3 x 10^16 m^-3

Numerical 4: Mass-action law

Question: If ni = 2 x 10^16 m^-3, calculate ni^2.

Formula: ni^2 = n x p

Solution:

ni^2 = (2 x 10^16)^2

ni^2 = 4 x 10^32 m^-6

Answer: 4 x 10^32 m^-6

Numerical 5: Find hole concentration

Question: A semiconductor has n = 8 x 10^20 m^-3 and ni = 2 x 10^16 m^-3. Find p at thermal equilibrium.

Formula: n x p = ni^2

Solution:

p = ni^2 / n

p = (2 x 10^16)^2 / (8 x 10^20)

p = 4 x 10^32 / 8 x 10^20

p = 5 x 10^11 m^-3

Answer: 5 x 10^11 m^-3

Numerical 6: Electron charge

Question: Find the magnitude of the charge of 5 electrons.

Given: q = 1.602 x 10^-19 C

Formula: Q = Nq

Solution:

Q = 5 x 1.602 x 10^-19

Q = 8.01 x 10^-19 C

Answer: Magnitude = 8.01 x 10^-19 C

Numerical 7: Conductivity

Question: Calculate conductivity when n = 2 x 10^20 m^-3, p = 1 x 10^20 m^-3, electron mobility = 0.14 m^2/Vs, hole mobility = 0.05 m^2/Vs and q = 1.6 x 10^-19 C.

Formula: sigma = q(n x mun + p x mup)

Solution:

sigma = 1.6 x 10^-19 [(2 x 10^20 x 0.14) + (1 x 10^20 x 0.05)]

sigma = 1.6 x 10^-19 (2.8 x 10^19 + 5 x 10^18)

sigma = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 3.3 x 10^19

sigma = 5.28 S/m

Answer: 5.28 S/m

Numerical 8: Find intrinsic concentration

Question: If n = 4 x 10^16 m^-3 and p = 9 x 10^16 m^-3 in an equilibrium semiconductor, calculate ni.

Formula: ni = square root of (n x p)

Solution:

ni = square root of (4 x 10^16 x 9 x 10^16)

ni = square root of (36 x 10^32)

ni = 6 x 10^16 m^-3

Answer: 6 x 10^16 m^-3

Numerical 9: Conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor

Question: An intrinsic semiconductor has ni = 2 x 10^16 m^-3, electron mobility = 0.12 m^2/Vs and hole mobility = 0.04 m^2/Vs. Calculate conductivity using q = 1.6 x 10^-19 C.

Formula: sigma = q(ni x mun + ni x mup)

Solution:

sigma = q x ni x (mun + mup)

sigma = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 2 x 10^16 x (0.12 + 0.04)

sigma = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 2 x 10^16 x 0.16

sigma = 5.12 x 10^-4 S/m

Answer: 5.12 x 10^-4 S/m

Numerical 10: Energy in joules

Question: Convert 0.7 eV into joules.

Formula: E(J) = E(eV) x 1.602 x 10^-19

Solution:

E = 0.7 x 1.602 x 10^-19

E = 1.1214 x 10^-19 J

Answer: 1.1214 x 10^-19 J

10. Quick Revision

  • Semiconductor conductivity lies between that of metals and insulators.
  • Electrons are negative charge carriers; holes behave as positive carriers.
  • Intrinsic semiconductor: pure semiconductor, n = p = ni.
  • Extrinsic semiconductor: semiconductor modified by doping.
  • N-type: electrons are majority carriers.
  • P-type: holes are majority carriers.
  • Mass-action law: n x p = ni^2.
  • A P-N junction forms a depletion region and a potential barrier.
  • Forward bias reduces the barrier; reverse bias increases it.
