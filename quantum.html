Quantum Theory

Quantum theory explains the behaviour of energy, electromagnetic radiation and microscopic particles. It introduced the ideas of energy quantization and wave-particle duality.

1. Inadequacy of Classical Physics

Meaning

Classical physics includes Newtonian mechanics, classical electromagnetism and classical thermodynamics. It successfully explains many macroscopic phenomena but cannot fully explain several microscopic phenomena.

Limitations of Classical Physics

  1. Black body radiation: Classical theory failed to explain the observed radiation spectrum at high frequencies.
  2. Photoelectric effect: Classical wave theory could not adequately explain the threshold frequency and the dependence of maximum electron kinetic energy on light frequency.
  3. Compton effect: The change in wavelength of scattered X-rays supports the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.
  4. Atomic spectra: Classical physics could not adequately explain the discrete energy levels of atoms.

These difficulties led to the development of quantum theory.

Concept of Quantization of Energy

In classical physics, energy is often treated as continuously variable. Quantum theory shows that certain systems can exchange energy in discrete amounts.

Max Planck proposed that the energy exchanged by an oscillator with radiation occurs in quanta.

Important Formula

Energy of one quantum:

E = hν

Allowed oscillator energy levels in Planck's model:

En = nhν

Where:

  • E = energy
  • h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10-34 J s
  • ν = frequency of radiation in hertz
  • n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

The main idea is that energy is exchanged in discrete packets rather than in arbitrary amounts in this model.

2. Particle Concept of Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic radiation exhibits both wave-like and particle-like behaviour. Its particle-like units are called photons.

Properties of a Photon

  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation.
  • Its energy is E = hν.
  • Its momentum is p = h/λ.
  • A photon has zero rest mass.
  • In vacuum, photons travel at the speed of light, c.

Important Photon Formulas

E = hν

Since ν = c/λ:

E = hc/λ

Photon momentum:

p = h/λ = E/c

Here c = 3 × 108 m/s is the speed of light in vacuum.

2.1 Black Body Radiation

A black body is an ideal body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of its wavelength or direction. When heated, it emits radiation whose spectrum depends on its absolute temperature.

Failure of Classical Theory

Classical theory predicted an unphysical increase in emitted energy at very high frequencies. This problem is known as the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Quantum Explanation

Planck proposed that energy is exchanged in discrete amounts:

E = hν

This assumption successfully explains the observed black body radiation spectrum.

Planck's Radiation Formula

For spectral energy density per unit frequency:

u(ν,T) = (8πhν3/c3) / [exp(hν/kT) - 1]

Where:

  • u(ν,T) = spectral energy density
  • h = Planck's constant
  • ν = frequency
  • c = speed of light
  • k = Boltzmann constant
  • T = absolute temperature in kelvin

For this syllabus, focus on the meaning of black body radiation, the failure of classical theory and Planck's energy quantization.

2.2 Photoelectric Effect

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency falls on its surface.

Important Observations

  1. For a given material, photoelectric emission occurs only when the incident frequency reaches or exceeds the threshold frequency.
  2. Increasing light intensity generally increases the photoelectric current when the frequency is above threshold.
  3. The maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on the frequency of incident light.
  4. Under suitable conditions, emission begins without a significant measurable delay.

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained the effect by assuming that one photon transfers its energy to one electron.

Energy conservation:

hν = φ + Kmax

Therefore:

Kmax = hν - φ

Where:

  • hν = energy of the incident photon
  • φ = work function of the material
  • Kmax = maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electron

Work function: The minimum energy required to remove an electron from a material's surface.

Threshold Frequency

At the threshold frequency, the emitted electron has zero maximum kinetic energy in the ideal relation.

hν0 = φ

Therefore:

ν0 = φ/h

Stopping Potential

The stopping potential is the reverse potential needed to stop even the fastest photoelectrons from reaching the collector.

eVs = Kmax

Combining the equations:

eVs = hν - φ

No derivation is required for the photoelectric effect according to your syllabus. Learn the concept, observations and formulas.

2.3 Compton Effect

The Compton effect is the increase in wavelength of an X-ray or photon when it scatters from an electron.

The effect can be explained by treating the photon as a particle that carries energy and momentum.

Compton Shift Formula

Δλ = λ' - λ

Δλ = [h/(mec)](1 - cos θ)

Where:

  • λ = initial photon wavelength
  • λ' = scattered photon wavelength
  • θ = photon scattering angle
  • me = electron rest mass
  • h = Planck's constant
  • c = speed of light

The quantity h/(mec) is called the Compton wavelength of the electron.

The electron Compton wavelength is approximately:

λC = 2.426 × 10-12 m

Special Cases

When θ = 0°:

Δλ = 0

When θ = 90°:

Δλ = λC

When θ = 180°:

Δλ = 2λC

No derivation of the Compton effect is required according to your syllabus.

3. Wave-Particle Duality

Wave-particle duality means that electromagnetic radiation and matter can show both wave-like and particle-like properties. Which behaviour is observed depends on the experiment.

Wave Nature of Light

  • Interference
  • Diffraction
  • Polarization

Particle Nature of Light

  • Photoelectric effect
  • Compton effect

Wave Nature of Matter

According to de Broglie, moving material particles such as electrons also have an associated wavelength.

Particle Nature of Matter

Electrons have measurable mass and charge and can produce localized detections in suitable experiments.

4. Phase Velocity and Group Velocity

Phase Velocity

Phase velocity is the speed at which a point of constant phase of a wave travels.

vp = ω/k

Since ω = 2πν and k = 2π/λ:

vp = νλ

Where:

  • vp = phase velocity
  • ω = angular frequency
  • k = wave number
  • ν = frequency
  • λ = wavelength

Group Velocity

Group velocity is the speed at which the envelope of a group of waves travels.

vg = dω/dk

For a wave packet representing a free, non-relativistic particle, the group velocity equals the particle's velocity.

Relationship for de Broglie Matter Waves

ω = E/ℏ

k = p/ℏ

For a free, non-relativistic particle:

E = p²/(2m)

Therefore, group velocity is:

vg = dω/dk = dE/dp = p/m = v

Here v is the particle's speed. This result applies to a free, non-relativistic matter wave.

5. de Broglie Hypothesis

Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving material particle has an associated wavelength.

de Broglie Wavelength

λ = h/p

For a non-relativistic particle, p = mv:

λ = h/(mv)

Where:

  • λ = de Broglie wavelength
  • h = Planck's constant
  • p = particle momentum
  • m = particle mass
  • v = particle speed

Wavelength in Terms of Kinetic Energy

For a non-relativistic particle:

K = mv²/2

Since p = √(2mK):

λ = h/√(2mK)

Electron Accelerated Through a Potential Difference

An electron starting from rest and accelerated through a potential difference V gains kinetic energy eV, assuming non-relativistic motion.

K = eV

Therefore:

λ = h/√(2meeV)

For an electron accelerated through V volts, a useful approximation is:

λ(nm) ≈ 1.227/√V

This approximation neglects relativistic effects and is suitable for relatively low electron accelerating voltages.

6. Davisson-Germer Experiment

Objective

The experiment was performed to investigate the wave nature of electrons and test de Broglie's hypothesis.

Experimental Setup

  • An electron gun produced an electron beam.
  • A potential difference accelerated the electrons.
  • The beam struck a nickel crystal.
  • A movable detector measured scattered electron intensity at different angles.

Observation

The scattered electron intensity showed diffraction maxima at particular angles.

Explanation

The regular atomic arrangement of the nickel crystal acted as a diffraction structure for the electron waves.

The wavelength obtained from diffraction was consistent with the de Broglie wavelength of the electrons.

Bragg's Law Used in the Experiment

nλ = 2d sin θ

Where:

  • n = order of diffraction
  • λ = wavelength
  • d = spacing between crystal planes
  • θ = Bragg angle

In the usual description of the experiment, the angle between the incident and scattered beams is twice the Bragg angle for the corresponding diffraction geometry.

Conclusion

The Davisson-Germer experiment provided direct experimental evidence for electron diffraction and supported the de Broglie hypothesis.

8. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Energy of a Photon

Question: Find the energy of a photon having frequency 5 × 1014 Hz.

Given:

h = 6.626 × 10-34 J s

ν = 5 × 1014 Hz

Formula: E = hν

Solution:

E = 6.626 × 10-34 × 5 × 1014

E = 3.313 × 10-19 J

Answer: 3.313 × 10-19 J

Numerical 2: Photon Energy from Wavelength

Question: Find the energy in eV of a photon having wavelength 500 nm.

Given:

h = 6.626 × 10-34 J s

c = 3 × 108 m/s

λ = 500 × 10-9 m

Formula: E = hc/λ

Solution:

E = (6.626 × 10-34 × 3 × 108) / (500 × 10-9)

E = 3.976 × 10-19 J

E = (3.976 × 10-19) / (1.602 × 10-19) eV

E ≈ 2.48 eV

Answer: Approximately 2.48 eV

Numerical 3: Threshold Frequency

Question: A material has a work function of 2 eV. Find its threshold frequency.

Given:

φ = 2 × 1.602 × 10-19 J

h = 6.626 × 10-34 J s

Formula: ν0 = φ/h

Solution:

ν0 = (2 × 1.602 × 10-19) / (6.626 × 10-34)

ν0 ≈ 4.84 × 1014 Hz

Answer: 4.84 × 1014 Hz

Numerical 4: Maximum Kinetic Energy

Question: Photons of energy 5 eV fall on a material with work function 2 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.

Formula: Kmax = E - φ

Solution:

Kmax = 5 - 2 = 3 eV

Answer: 3 eV

Numerical 5: Stopping Potential

Question: The maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is 2.5 eV. Find the stopping potential.

Formula: eVs = Kmax

When kinetic energy is expressed in eV, its numerical value equals the stopping potential in volts.

Solution:

Vs = 2.5 V

Answer: 2.5 V

Numerical 6: de Broglie Wavelength

Question: An electron moves at 2 × 106 m/s. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

Given:

h = 6.626 × 10-34 J s

me = 9.11 × 10-31 kg

v = 2 × 106 m/s

Formula: λ = h/(mv)

Solution:

λ = (6.626 × 10-34) / (9.11 × 10-31 × 2 × 106)

λ ≈ 3.64 × 10-10 m

Answer: 3.64 × 10-10 m

Numerical 7: Electron Accelerated Through a Potential

Question: Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V.

Formula:

λ(nm) ≈ 1.227/√V

Solution:

λ = 1.227/√100 nm

λ = 1.227/10 nm

λ = 0.1227 nm

Answer: 0.1227 nm

Numerical 8: Compton Shift

Question: Find the Compton wavelength shift when a photon is scattered through 90 degrees. Take the electron Compton wavelength as 2.426 × 10-12 m.

Formula:

Δλ = λC(1 - cos θ)

Solution:

Δλ = 2.426 × 10-12(1 - cos 90°)

Since cos 90° = 0:

Δλ = 2.426 × 10-12 m

Answer: 2.426 × 10-12 m

Numerical 9: Phase Velocity

Question: A wave has angular frequency 20 rad/s and wave number 4 rad/m. Find its phase velocity.

Formula: vp = ω/k

Solution:

vp = 20/4 = 5 m/s

Answer: 5 m/s

Numerical 10: Bragg's Law

Question: X-rays of wavelength 0.154 nm are reflected from crystal planes separated by 0.20 nm. Find the first-order Bragg angle.

Given:

λ = 0.154 nm

d = 0.20 nm

n = 1

Formula: nλ = 2d sin θ

Solution:

sin θ = nλ/(2d)

sin θ = 0.154/(2 × 0.20)

sin θ = 0.385

θ = sin-1(0.385)

θ ≈ 22.6°

Answer: Bragg angle ≈ 22.6°
